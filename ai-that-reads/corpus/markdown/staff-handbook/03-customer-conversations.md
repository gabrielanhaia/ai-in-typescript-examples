# Staff handbook — 3. Customer conversations

Last updated: 6 January 2026
Applies to: Customer Operations, trade counter, workshop

## The one rule

If you do not know, say you do not know, and then find out.

Everything else in this section is a consequence of that sentence. A customer
who is told "I'm not sure, give me an hour and I'll check" comes back. A
customer who is told something confident and wrong does not, and we usually
find out about it in a review rather than in a ticket.

## Prices

Do not quote a price in a conversation. Point the customer at the product page.

The reason is not policy for its own sake: the page is the price, the page
updates, and a price quoted in an email on Tuesday is a price we may be held to
on Friday. This applies to email, to the phone, and to the counter.

Workshop labour is the exception, and only from the current price list, and
only as a quotation on a job sheet.

## Compatibility questions

"Will this fit my bike?" is the most common technical question and the easiest
one to get wrong.

Ask for three things before answering: the make and model of the bike, the year,
and a photograph of the area in question. Two of the three is usually not
enough — the year is what changes the standard, and the photograph is what
tells you whether the previous owner changed something.

If the answer is not certain, say it is not certain, and offer the return
policy as the safety net rather than guessing. A part returned unfitted costs us
a picking slot. A part fitted on bad advice costs us a customer.

## What we do not advise on

- Whether a damaged frame or component is safe to keep riding. Refer it to the
  workshop for inspection, and say plainly that we are not able to judge it from
  a photograph.
- Anything medical, including bike fit as a treatment for pain.
- Anything about another retailer's product, warranty or service.
- Legal questions about a road incident.

Declining is not unhelpful as long as it comes with a next step. "We can't tell
from a photo, but the workshop will look at it free of charge" is a decline and
a route in the same sentence.

## Tone

Plain, short, and without the apparatus. No "I completely understand your
frustration". No exclamation marks in a complaint. No emoji anywhere.

Use the customer's words for the part they are describing, then use ours once
alongside it. A customer who says "the disc thing that the pads sit in" is
describing a caliper, and answering with "the caliper" and nothing else makes
them feel stupid for the rest of the conversation.

## Complaints

Acknowledge within one working day. Say what happened, in one sentence, without
blaming a carrier or a supplier by name. Say what we are going to do. Give a
date.

Do not offer a goodwill discount without a team lead. Do not tell a customer
what another team is going to decide before that team has decided it.

If a complaint has been round twice and is not moving, hand it to a team lead
rather than sending a third version of the same email. The third email is what
turns a complaint into a review.

## Abuse

Nobody has to absorb abuse. On the phone: one warning, then end the call. By
email: stop replying and pass the thread to a team lead. At the counter: step
back, get the workshop manager, and do not engage.

Record it. A customer who has done it twice is a customer we stop trading with,
and that decision needs a record to rest on.

## Handovers

Every conversation that is not finished gets a note in the ticket that a
stranger could act on. Names of parts, order number, what has been promised,
what is still open, and when the customer expects to hear back.

The test is whether somebody who has never read the thread could pick it up
cold. "Customer chasing" fails that test. "Customer chasing warranty decision on
BRK-2200 rear caliper, order 481-2209, workshop inspection booked Thursday,
promised a call by Friday" passes it.
