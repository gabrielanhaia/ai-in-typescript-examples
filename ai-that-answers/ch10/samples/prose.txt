A bicycle wheel is a structure that works entirely in tension. The rim does not
sit on top of the spokes the way a cart wheel sits on its wooden spokes; it hangs
from the ones above the hub while the ones below go slack under load. Every spoke
is pulled tight when the wheel is built, and riding the bike simply moves that
tension around the circle rather than adding to it. This is why a wheel that has
lost a single spoke can still be ridden home gently, and why a wheel that has been
built with uneven tension will go out of true long before anything actually breaks.
It is also why truing a wheel is a matter of adjusting tension rather than bending
metal back into shape: you turn a nipple a quarter of a turn, the rim moves a
fraction of a millimetre toward that spoke, and the whole structure settles into a
new balance. Wheelbuilders talk about a wheel being stress-relieved, which means
the spokes have been squeezed hard enough after lacing that they have taken their
final set and will not creak or unwind in the first hundred miles. None of this is
visible from the outside, which is the interesting part. A good wheel and a bad
wheel look identical on a shop floor, and the difference only shows up after a
winter of potholes.
